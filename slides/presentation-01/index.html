Answers · Question 1

What do we mean by linear regression?

Generally, linear regression is any regression model that is linear in its parameters.

$$ \hat{y}=\theta_0+\theta_1\phi_1(x)+\cdots+\theta_d\phi_d(x). $$

The functions of the inputs can be nonlinear, for example $x^2$ or $\sin(x)$.

It is also reasonable to call a model using only linear relationships between inputs and outputs “linear regression”, and the broader case “linear regression with nonlinear basis functions”.

The answer to this depends somewhat on the notation used. Generally, linear regression is any regression model that is linear in the parameters. However, it is also reasonable to define linear regression as a model that models only linear relationships between input and output features and define the case that uses basis functions as linear regression with non-linear basis functions.

Answers · Question 1

When would I use it?

Using the broader definition, any regression task with a linear relationship, or one we can reasonably express using basis functions, is a candidate.

  • Predicting a continuous value, such as house price or temperature.
  • Modelling relationships between variables.
  • Describing trends in data.
Using the first case, any task that is either a linear relationship or can be transformed reasonably into a linear relationship can be solved with linear regression. The examples illustrate this without changing the distinction in the written answer.

Answers · Question 1

When would it not be suitable?

  • When the relationship cannot reasonably be represented by a linear combination of the chosen basis functions.
  • When assumptions needed for how we use the model are violated.
  • In its basic form, for classification: it predicts continuous values rather than categories.

The written answer mentions constant error variance, independent errors, and normal errors. Normality concerns classical inference, rather than being required to fit least squares.

The written answer says that inherently nonlinear tasks that cannot be transformed reasonably, violations of assumptions such as homoscedasticity, independence and normality of errors, and classification can make basic linear regression unsuitable. Which assumptions matter depends on whether we want predictions, standard errors, confidence intervals, or hypothesis tests. Noise and outliers are modelling concerns more generally.

Answers · Question 2a

Minimising the sum of squared errors

$$ \hat{y}_i=mx_i+c,\qquad L(m,c)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-mx_i-c)^2. $$

Set both partial derivatives to zero:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial m}&=-2\sum_i x_i(y_i-mx_i-c)=0,\\[0.5em] \frac{\partial L}{\partial c}&=-2\sum_i(y_i-mx_i-c)=0. \end{aligned} $$
This is the sum-of-squares loss from the written answer. Differentiating with respect to both slope and intercept gives two equations to solve together.

Answers · Question 2a

Solving for the intercept

Write the sample means as:

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i,\qquad \bar{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i. $$

The second equation gives:

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_i y_i-m\sum_i x_i-nc&=0,\\ n\bar{y}-mn\bar{x}-nc&=0,\\ \boxed{c=\bar{y}-m\bar{x}}.& \end{aligned} $$
The fitted line passes through the point of sample means. Substitute this expression for c into the first derivative equation next.

Answers · Question 2a

Substituting to find the slope

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_i x_i y_i-m\sum_i x_i^2-(\bar{y}-m\bar{x})\sum_i x_i&=0,\\[0.6em] m\left(\sum_i x_i^2-n\bar{x}^2\right)&=\sum_i x_i y_i-n\bar{x}\bar{y}. \end{aligned} $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{m=\frac{\sum_i x_i y_i-n\bar{x}\bar{y}}{\sum_i x_i^2-n\bar{x}^2} =\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2}}. $$

This is the unique minimum when $\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2>0$: the ages must not all be identical.

These are both equivalent forms of the slope in the written answer. The centred form is also covariance divided by variance when the same normalisation is used for both.

Answers · Question 3a

The model and objective

$X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}$, $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$.

There are $n$ observations, $d$ features, and an intercept column.

$$ \hat{y}=X\theta,\qquad L(\theta)=\frac12\lVert y-X\theta\rVert_2^2. $$

The factor $\tfrac12$ makes the derivative simpler. Omitting it gives the same minimiser.

There are two ways to go about the derivation: expand the squared norm and differentiate each term, or differentiate the squared norm directly. Both lead to the same normal equations.

Answers · Question 3a

Writing out the matrices

$$ X=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1d}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2d}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{nd} \end{bmatrix},\quad \theta=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\\theta_1\\\vdots\\\theta_d\end{bmatrix},\quad y=\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n\end{bmatrix}. $$

The first column gives the intercept. Multiplying each row by $\theta$ gives one prediction.

Answers · Question 3a · Method 1

Expanding the squared norm

$$ \begin{aligned} L(\theta)&=\frac12(y-X\theta)^T(y-X\theta)\\[0.5em] &=\frac12\left(y^Ty-y^TX\theta-\theta^TX^Ty+\theta^TX^TX\theta\right)\\[0.5em] &=\frac12\left(y^Ty-2\theta^TX^Ty+\theta^TX^TX\theta\right). \end{aligned} $$

The two cross-terms are equal because they are scalars. We can combine them.

Answers · Question 3a · Method 1

Why are the cross-terms equal?

The term $y^TX\theta$ is a $1\times1$ scalar. Its transpose is the same value:

$$ \begin{aligned} y^TX\theta&=(y^TX\theta)^T\\ &=\theta^TX^T(y^T)^T\\ &=\theta^TX^Ty. \end{aligned} $$

Taking a transpose reverses the order: $(ABC)^T=C^TB^TA^T$.

Answers · Question 3a · Method 1

Differentiating the expanded terms

$$ L(\theta)=\frac12\left(y^Ty-2\theta^TX^Ty+\theta^TX^TX\theta\right). $$

Differentiate with respect to $\theta$ and set the gradient to zero:

$$ \nabla_\theta L=-X^Ty+X^TX\theta=0. $$ $$ \boxed{X^TX\theta=X^Ty}. $$

Answers · Question 3a · Method 2

Differentiating the norm directly

$$ \nabla_\theta L=\nabla_\theta\frac12\lVert y-X\theta\rVert_2^2 =\nabla_\theta\frac12(y-X\theta)^T(y-X\theta). $$

Using the derivative of the residual vector, $y-X\theta$:

$$ \nabla_\theta L=-X^T(y-X\theta)=0. $$

Rearranging gives the same normal equations:

$$ \boxed{X^TX\theta=X^Ty}. $$
This is the second method in the written answer. The derivative of one-half the squared Euclidean norm of a vector is the vector itself; the residual's derivative contributes the factor minus X transpose.

Answers · Question 3a

Solving the normal equations

Both methods lead to:

$$ X^TX\theta=X^Ty. $$

If $X^TX$ is invertible, multiply both sides by its inverse:

$$ \boxed{\hat{\theta}=(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty}. $$

Answers · Question 3b

When does the inverse formula fail?

When $X^TX$ is singular, it has no inverse.

$$ \operatorname{rank}(X)<d+1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^TX\text{ is singular}. $$
  • One column is an exact linear combination of others.
  • There are more parameters than observations: $n<d+1$.

Least-squares solutions still exist, but the parameters are not unique. It is the inverse formula that fails.

Answers · Question 3c · Fix 1

Adding regularisation

Add a penalty on the weights, with $\lambda>0$:

$$ L_\lambda(\theta)=\frac12\lVert y-X\theta\rVert_2^2+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2. $$

Setting the gradient to zero gives:

$$ X^TX\theta+\lambda\theta=X^Ty. $$ $$ \boxed{\hat{\theta}_\lambda=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty}. $$
The written answer uses one-half on the residual loss but lambda times the weight norm squared, then writes a gradient with lambda theta. Strictly that penalty differentiates to 2 lambda theta. Here the penalty is lambda over two so the stated normal equations and solution are consistent. This only changes the convention for naming the tuning parameter. As in the written formula, the identity matrix here penalises every parameter, including the intercept.

Answers · Question 3c · Fix 1

Why does adding to the diagonal help?

For any nonzero vector $v$ and $\lambda>0$:

$$ v^T(X^TX+\lambda I)v=\lVert Xv\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert v\rVert_2^2>0. $$

The matrix is positive definite and therefore invertible, giving a unique solution.

Increasing $\lambda$ penalises large weights more strongly. This improves stability, but can reduce how well the model fits the data.

The written answer explains the diagonal adjustment and the tradeoff controlled by lambda. The quadratic-form identity shows why a positive lambda fixes singularity when all parameters are penalised.

Answers · Question 3c · Regularisation example

Two identical features

$$ \hat{y}=\theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2. $$

If $x_1=x_2=x$, then:

$$ \hat{y}=\theta_0+(\theta_1+\theta_2)x. $$

Only the sum $\theta_1+\theta_2$ affects their contribution. The individual weights are not identified by the data.

Other ways to fit the parameters

Out of the scope of this course.

3. Use a different optimisation algorithm. Gradient descent does not require the inverse of $X^TX$.

Answers · Question 3c · Fixes 4, 5 and 6

Changing the data or representation

  • 4. Increase the number of samples. More informative observations can make full column rank possible.
  • 5. Use feature selection. Remove redundant columns to reduce the number of parameters.
  • 6. Use dimensionality reduction. For example, PCA can represent the inputs with fewer components.

PCA is out of scope here. More samples alone cannot fix columns that are always exact copies of each other.

The written answer lists increasing the number of samples, reducing the number of features through selection, and using dimensionality reduction such as PCA. Each can help when it addresses the source of rank deficiency; none is an unconditional guarantee for arbitrary data.
