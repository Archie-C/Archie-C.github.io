Information Theory.

Information theory provides the framework for quantifying information, uncertainty, and the similarity between probability distributions. In deep learning, it gives us the mathematical foundations for training objectives, such as the cross-entropy loss used in classification.

What this note covers

  • Self-information as a measure of surprise

  • Shannon Entropy as the expected surprise of a distribution

  • Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence (Relative Entropy) and its asymmetry

  • Cross-Entropy as a classification training objective

  • Mutual Information as shared uncertainty reduction

For a detailed treatment, see Chapter 6 of Mathematics for Machine Learning.

Self-Information (Surprise)

The core intuition of information theory is that learning that an unlikely event has occurred is more informative than learning that a likely event has occurred. If someone tells you "it is raining in the Sahara desert," that carries far more information than "it is raining in Seattle."

We quantify the information content (or surprise) of an event $x$ with the self-information $I(x)$:

$$ I(x) = -\log_2 P(x) $$

Let's unpack this formula:

  • The Logarithm: We use a logarithm so that information is additive. If two independent events $x$ and $y$ occur, their joint probability is $P(x)P(y)$, and their combined surprise is $I(x) + I(y) = -\log_2(P(x)P(y)) = -\log_2 P(x) - \log_2 P(y)$.

  • The Base: When using base 2, the unit of information is the bit (or Shannon). A flip of a fair coin has a probability of $0.5$, yielding $I(\text{heads}) = -\log_2(0.5) = 1$ bit of surprise. If we use the natural logarithm (base $e$), the unit is the nat.

  • The Negative Sign: Since probabilities are between $0$ and $1$, their logarithms are negative. The leading minus sign ensures that surprise is a positive number. As probability $P(x)$ approaches $1$ (certainty), the surprise $I(x)$ drops to $0$. As $P(x)$ approaches $0$ (improbability), surprise goes to infinity.

Shannon Entropy

While self-information measures the surprise of a single event, the Shannon Entropy $H(X)$ measures the average surprise of an entire probability distribution $P$ over a random variable $X$:

$$ H(X) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[I(x)] = -\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log_2 P(x_i) $$

Entropy measures the uncertainty of a distribution. For a discrete variable with $n$ possible outcomes:

  • Minimum Entropy ($H(X) = 0$): Occurs when one outcome has a probability of $1.0$ and all others have $0$. There is zero uncertainty, so the average surprise is zero.

  • Maximum Entropy ($H(X) = \log_2 n$): Occurs when all $n$ outcomes are equally likely (uniform distribution). This is the state of maximum uncertainty.

Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence

In machine learning, we often approximate a true, unknown distribution $P$ using a model's predicted distribution $Q$. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence (or Relative Entropy) measures the average extra surprise we experience if we encode messages from $P$ using the distribution $Q$:

$$ D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log_2 \frac{P(x_i)}{Q(x_i)} = \sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log_2 P(x_i) - \sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log_2 Q(x_i) $$

We can write this as an expectation over $P$:

$$ D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[\log_2 \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right] $$

The KL divergence has several critical properties:

  • Non-negativity ($D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) \ge 0$): It is always greater than or equal to zero. It equals zero if and only if $P = Q$ everywhere.

  • Asymmetry ($D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) \neq D_{KL}(Q \parallel P)$): Because of this asymmetry, the KL divergence is not a true distance metric. The order matters: $D_{KL}(P \parallel Q)$ is the expectation of the difference relative to the distribution $P$, which weights the surprise differently than $Q$.

Cross-Entropy

By expanding the KL divergence formula, we can isolate a term called the Cross-Entropy $H(P, Q)$:

$$ H(P, Q) = -\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log_2 Q(x_i) $$

This allows us to write the fundamental relationship between these three quantities:

$$ H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) $$

This equation has a beautiful interpretation: the total average surprise of using distribution $Q$ to model data from $P$ (the Cross-Entropy) is equal to the inherent uncertainty of $P$ itself (the Entropy) plus the penalty of using the incorrect distribution $Q$ (the KL Divergence).

In deep learning classification, $P$ is the true label (a one-hot vector where the correct class has probability $1.0$, meaning $H(P) = 0$), and $Q$ is our model's predicted softmax distribution. Because $H(P)$ is constant with respect to the model parameters, minimising the Cross-Entropy loss $H(P, Q)$ is mathematically identical to minimising the KL divergence $D_{KL}(P \parallel Q)$ between the labels and predictions.

Mutual Information

The Mutual Information $I(X; Y)$ measures the amount of information shared between two random variables $X$ and $Y$. It quantifies how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other:

$$ I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) $$

where $H(X|Y)$ is the conditional entropy (the remaining uncertainty of $X$ given that we know $Y$). We can also write it as the KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of the marginals:

$$ I(X; Y) = D_{KL}(P(x, y) \parallel P(x)P(y)) $$

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, their joint probability factors to $P(x, y) = P(x)P(y)$, yielding a mutual information of $0$.

Geometric Connection: Entropy and Tangents

There is a striking geometric relationship between Entropy and Cross-Entropy for binary distributions. Consider a coin with true probability of heads $p$ and model probability $q$. The entropy is the curve $H(p)$, and the cross-entropy is $H(p, q)$.

Because the entropy curve $H(p)$ is concave, the linear approximation (the tangent line) to the entropy curve at any point $p = q$ is exactly the cross-entropy function $H(p, q)$! Since a concave curve lies entirely below its tangent lines, the cross-entropy line always sits above the entropy curve. The vertical gap between the tangent line (Cross-Entropy) and the curve (Entropy) at any coordinate $p$ is precisely the KL divergence $D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) \ge 0$.
