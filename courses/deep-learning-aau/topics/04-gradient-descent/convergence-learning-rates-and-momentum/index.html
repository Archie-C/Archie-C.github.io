Convergence, learning rates and momentum.

Why can gradient descent be slow or unstable, even when every update uses the direction of steepest local decrease?

What this note covers

  • Convergence on a quadratic loss
  • Local curvature, Hessian eigenvalues and conditioning
  • Momentum and look-ahead gradients
  • Learning-rate schedules and optimisation behaviour

Convergence of gradient descent

In the previous notes we used the update $\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_t-\eta\nabla E(\mathbf{w}_t)$. Now we can ask what happens when we repeat it. A sequence of parameters converges to $\mathbf{w}^*$ if, as $t$ grows, the distance $\|\mathbf{w}_t-\mathbf{w}^*\|$ approaches zero. The loss values can converge without the parameters converging: for example, parameters can move along a flat valley of constant loss. Even if the parameters converge to a stationary point, that point need not be a minimum. We also care about the rate: how quickly do the errors become small?

The negative gradient gives the direction of greatest decrease for a sufficiently small displacement. A finite step can cross a valley and end higher than it began, because the slope changes along the way. To see precisely when this happens, consider a quadratic with curvature $\lambda>0$ and minimum $w^*$:

$$E(w)=\frac12\lambda(w-w^*)^2,\qquad \frac{dE}{dw}=\lambda(w-w^*).$$

Subtracting $w^*$ from the update gives $w_{t+1}-w^*=(1-\eta\lambda)(w_t-w^*)$. The same multiplier is applied at each iteration, so repeated substitution gives:

$$\boxed{w_t-w^*=(1-\eta\lambda)^t(w_0-w^*).}$$

For a nonzero initial error, the parameter converges to the minimum exactly when $|1-\eta\lambda|<1$, or $0<\eta<2/\lambda$. If $0<\eta\lambda<1$, the error keeps its sign and shrinks. At $\eta\lambda=1$ the minimum is reached in one step. If $1<\eta\lambda<2$, the sign alternates while the magnitude shrinks: the iterates cross the minimum. At $\eta\lambda=2$ they oscillate indefinitely, unless they start at the minimum; above it they diverge. At $\eta=0$ they do not move.

This is geometric or linear convergence: each step multiplies the absolute parameter error by the same factor $q=|1-\eta\lambda|$, rather than removing a fixed distance. The quadratic loss above its minimum is proportional to the square of that error, so it is multiplied by $q^2$ per step. These are exact results for this quadratic, not universal learning-rate bounds for a neural network. They explain the oscillation and divergence observed in the first note.

Local quadratic approximation and the Hessian

Neural network losses are not generally quadratic. Nevertheless, a smooth function can be approximated near a point by a second-order Taylor expansion. For a small displacement $\Delta\mathbf{w}$:

$$E(\mathbf{w}+\Delta\mathbf{w})\approx E(\mathbf{w})+\nabla E(\mathbf{w})^\top\Delta\mathbf{w}+\frac12\Delta\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf H(\mathbf{w})\Delta\mathbf{w}.$$

The gradient describes the local slope. The Hessian collects the second derivatives, describing how that slope changes:

$$\mathbf H=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_1^2}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_1\partial w_M}\\\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_M\partial w_1}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_M^2}\end{pmatrix}.$$

When the second derivatives are continuous, mixed partial derivatives agree, so $\mathbf H$ is symmetric. The quadratic term depends on the direction of the displacement: the same distance in two directions can produce different changes in loss. This is why a local bowl can have elongated rather than circular contours. The approximation is local; farther away, curvature can change and higher-order terms can matter.

At a stationary point $\nabla E(\mathbf w^*)=\mathbf0$, so the linear term vanishes. Writing $\mathbf H=\mathbf H(\mathbf w^*)$, nearby values are approximately

$$E(\mathbf w)\approx E(\mathbf w^*)+\frac12(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^*)^\top\mathbf H(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^*).$$

If $\mathbf H$ is positive definite, the quadratic term is positive for every nonzero displacement and the stationary point is a strict local minimum. Negative definite curvature gives a strict local maximum. An indefinite Hessian has both rising and falling directions, giving a saddle. Positive semidefinite curvature alone does not establish a strict minimum: $E(w)=0$ is flat everywhere. Conversely, a strict minimum need not have a positive definite Hessian: $E(w)=w^4$ has a strict minimum at zero but second derivative zero there. In such degenerate cases the quadratic term alone cannot settle the classification.

Consider the exact quadratic

$$E(w_1,w_2)=\tfrac12(w_1-2)^2+10(w_2+1)^2,\quad \nabla E=\begin{pmatrix}w_1-2\\20(w_2+1)\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&20\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its minimum is $(2,-1)^\top$. The diagonal entries say that the slope changes twenty times as rapidly in the $w_2$ direction as in the $w_1$ direction. The contours are correspondingly narrow across $w_2$. The figure lets you change both curvatures: each panel uses the same objective, $\frac12\lambda_1(w_1-2)^2+\frac12\lambda_2(w_2+1)^2$.

Curvature, eigenvalues and slow convergence

The bowl above is convex and has a unique minimum, yet gradient descent can still be slow. Its Hessian has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_2=20$, with eigenvectors along the $w_1$ and $w_2$ axes. These eigenvalues give the curvatures in those directions. Away from the centre, the much steeper cross-valley component can dominate the gradient. A step then points mostly across the narrow valley, rather than along it.

Let's start at $\mathbf w_0=(0,0)^\top$ with $\eta=0.08$. The initial gradient is $(-2,20)^\top$, and the first step gives $(0.16,-1.6)^\top$. Recomputing the gradient at each point:

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla E(\mathbf w_1)&=(-1.84,-12)^\top,&\quad\mathbf w_2&=(0.16,-1.6)^\top-0.08(-1.84,-12)^\top=(0.3072,-0.64)^\top,\\\nabla E(\mathbf w_2)&=(-1.6928,7.2)^\top,&\quad\mathbf w_3&=(0.3072,-0.64)^\top-0.08(-1.6928,7.2)^\top=(0.442624,-1.216)^\top.\end{aligned}$$

The second coordinate crosses $-1$ on each step, while the first moves steadily but slowly towards $2$. The learning rate must be small enough for the steep direction, and that limits progress in the shallow direction.

To see this in general, take an exact positive definite quadratic with symmetric Hessian $\mathbf H$. Its orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf u_i$ form a basis and satisfy $\mathbf H\mathbf u_i=\lambda_i\mathbf u_i$, with $\lambda_i>0$. Write the displacement as $\mathbf w_t-\mathbf w^*=\sum_i\alpha_{i,t}\mathbf u_i$. Since $\nabla E=\mathbf H(\mathbf w_t-\mathbf w^*)$, substituting into the update gives

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf w_{t+1}-\mathbf w^*&=(\mathbf I-\eta\mathbf H)\sum_i\alpha_{i,t}\mathbf u_i\\&=\sum_i(1-\eta\lambda_i)\alpha_{i,t}\mathbf u_i.\end{aligned}$$

Each direction therefore evolves independently:

$$\alpha_{i,t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda_i)\alpha_{i,t},\qquad \boxed{\alpha_{i,t}=(1-\eta\lambda_i)^t\alpha_{i,0}.}$$

Each component has its own convergence factor. To converge from every starting point, all factors must have magnitude below one, giving $0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$. A particular starting point can lack a component in an unstable direction, but that is not a general guarantee. For the example, the factors are $1-0.08(1)=0.92$ and $1-0.08(20)=-0.6$. The first error retains 92% of its size each step; the second changes sign and retains 60%. This is exactly the trajectory calculated above.

The condition number of a positive definite Hessian is $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$. Here $\kappa=20/1=20$. A large ratio describes a poorly conditioned bowl: the steepest direction constrains the step while the shallowest can remain slow. It compares curvatures, not gradient magnitudes; gradient size also depends on the current position. For a general loss, a Hessian and its curvature can change from point to point, and need not be positive definite.

Momentum

Across a narrow valley, successive gradients can alternate in sign; along it, their shallow components can keep pointing in the same direction. Momentum uses previous updates as well as the current gradient. In the classical form, with velocity $\mathbf v_t$ representing the previous update,

$$\mathbf v_{t+1}=\mu\mathbf v_t-\eta\nabla E(\mathbf w_t),\qquad \boxed{\mathbf w_{t+1}=\mathbf w_t+\mathbf v_{t+1}.}$$

The new update combines a fraction $\mu$ of the previous update with a negative-gradient step. If $\mu=0$, this is ordinary gradient descent. Consistent gradient contributions accumulate along a direction; alternating contributions can partially cancel across it. This does not mean that oscillation always disappears: velocity can carry the parameters across the valley and beyond the minimum.

Let $\mathbf g_t=\nabla E(\mathbf w_t)$ and start with $\mathbf v_0=\mathbf0$. Repeated substitution gives

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf v_{t+1}&=-\eta\mathbf g_t+\mu(-\eta\mathbf g_{t-1}+\mu\mathbf v_{t-1})\\&=-\eta\left(\mathbf g_t+\mu\mathbf g_{t-1}+\cdots+\mu^t\mathbf g_0\right)=-\eta\sum_{k=0}^t\mu^k\mathbf g_{t-k}.\end{aligned}$$

For $0\leq\mu<1$, older gradients have exponentially decreasing weights. If the gradient stays approximately constant for many iterations, the geometric sum approaches $1/(1-\mu)$, so the update approaches $\mathbf v\approx-\eta\mathbf g/(1-\mu)$. This resembles a larger effective learning rate in that direction. It is a constant-gradient approximation, not an equivalence on an arbitrary curved surface, where gradients change with position.

A larger $\mu$ retains past updates for longer. A value of $0.9$ is a common starting choice, not a guarantee of good behaviour. Greater persistence can speed progress but can also cause overshooting or instability. The learning rate and momentum coefficient act together, so changing either can require reconsidering the other. Worked example

Return to $E(w)=(w-3)^2$, with $w_0=0$, $v_0=0$, $\eta=0.1$ and $\mu=0.8$. The first three iterations are:

$$\begin{aligned}g_0&=2(0-3)=-6,&v_1&=0.8(0)-0.1(-6)=0.6,&w_1&=0.6,\\g_1&=2(0.6-3)=-4.8,&v_2&=0.8(0.6)-0.1(-4.8)=0.96,&w_2&=1.56,\\g_2&=2(1.56-3)=-2.88,&v_3&=0.8(0.96)-0.1(-2.88)=1.056,&w_3&=2.616.\end{aligned}$$

Ordinary gradient descent from the same start and learning rate gives $0.6$, $1.08$ and $1.464$. Momentum adds a retained part of the preceding update, producing larger steps here even as the gradient decreases. It subsequently crosses $3$: the fourth update is $v_4=0.9216$, giving $w_4=3.5376$. The loss can increase on a momentum step; it need not decrease at every iteration.

Nesterov momentum

Classical momentum evaluates the gradient at the current position, then combines it with the retained velocity. Nesterov momentum instead evaluates it at a look-ahead position in the direction the existing momentum would carry us:

$$\mathbf v_{t+1}=\mu\mathbf v_t-\eta\nabla E(\mathbf w_t+\mu\mathbf v_t),\qquad \mathbf w_{t+1}=\mathbf w_t+\mathbf v_{t+1}.$$

The look-ahead point $\mathbf w_t+\mu\mathbf v_t$ is where the retained part of the velocity alone would take us. Its gradient can signal a change in slope before the parameters reach that point. Both methods add the retained velocity and a gradient contribution, but they evaluate that contribution at different positions. On a curved surface the two gradients differ; the look-ahead can give a useful correction, although it is not universally superior.

Learning-rate schedules

A fixed learning rate must serve both the early stages, when substantial movement can help, and later stages, when smaller updates may refine the solution. With stochastic or mini-batch gradients, noise adds another reason to reduce step sizes: a fixed rate can keep parameters fluctuating around a minimum even when the average gradient there is zero. A suitably decreasing rate can permit more precise convergence under appropriate assumptions, but a schedule alone does not guarantee it.

Let the learning rate depend on iteration: $\mathbf w_{t+1}=\mathbf w_t-\eta_t\nabla E(\mathbf w_t)$. A constant schedule $\eta_t=\eta_0$ is simple, but cannot change the balance between speed and precision during training.

For linear decay, choose a duration $T$ and interpolate uniformly:

$$\eta_t=\eta_{\mathrm{start}}+\frac{t}{T}(\eta_{\mathrm{end}}-\eta_{\mathrm{start}}),\quad 0\leq t\leq T.$$

After $T$, hold the rate at $\eta_{\mathrm{end}}$. With start $0.1$, end $0.01$ and $T=100$, the rates at $t=0,50,100$ are $0.1$, $0.055$ and $0.01$ respectively.

Exponential decay uses $\eta_t=\eta_0\gamma^t$, where $0<\gamma<1$. Each step multiplies the previous rate by $\gamma$: the absolute decrease shrinks over time, unlike the fixed decrement of linear decay. A power-law schedule is

$$\eta_t=\frac{\eta_0}{(1+t/s)^p},\qquad s>0,\quad p>0.$$

The scale $s$ controls when substantial decay occurs and $p$ controls its strength. Its long tail decreases more slowly than an exponential with a fixed decay factor. Neither needs to reach zero at a finite step.

Schedules can use optimisation steps or epochs as their clock. These are not equivalent when batch size changes: a different batch size changes the number of updates per epoch. Other choices include cosine decay and warmup, in which the rate is initially increased before the main schedule. Each introduces choices such as a duration or decay factor. Decaying too quickly can stop progress before a good region is reached; an excessively small final rate can make further training ineffective. The appropriate schedule depends on the problem and the optimiser.

Understanding optimisation behaviour

These results give us several ways to interpret a training curve. Slow but steady improvement can reflect a small learning rate or a poorly conditioned valley, where a rate constrained by steep curvature makes little progress along a shallow direction. A plateau can occur where gradients are small, including along a low-curvature direction; it does not by itself identify a minimum.

Oscillating parameters can result from crossing a high-curvature valley with a large step, or from retained momentum. Stochastic gradient noise can also make updates fluctuate. Compare full-batch and mini-batch behaviour, inspect parameter paths and consider whether reducing the rate or momentum changes the pattern. Oscillating parameters do not necessarily imply oscillating loss: symmetric points on opposite sides of a quadratic can have equal loss. Conversely, noisy loss alone cannot identify which mechanism is responsible.

Diverging loss can occur when updates become so large that they move into increasingly high-loss regions. Fast initial improvement followed by slow progress can reflect shrinking gradients, changing local curvature or a decaying learning rate. These explanations can coexist and the quadratic analysis does not establish which one applies to a particular network.

Monitoring training loss tells us what the objective is doing, but not the whole cause. The gradient norm measures local slope; the update norm measures actual movement and also depends on the learning rate and, with momentum, velocity. A small update can come from a small gradient or a small learning rate; a substantial momentum update can persist even when the current gradient is small. Looking at these quantities together helps distinguish possible explanations.

Summary

For a positive definite quadratic, eigenvalues determine how quickly errors in different directions change. A large curvature ratio can force ordinary gradient descent to make slow progress along a valley. Momentum combines past gradient contributions, while Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient at a look-ahead point. Learning-rate schedules change update sizes during training. These ideas describe useful mechanisms, but the exact quadratic convergence bounds do not automatically extend to non-convex network losses.

Next, adaptive optimisation methods such as RMSProp and Adam will use information from previous gradients to adjust updates for individual parameters.
