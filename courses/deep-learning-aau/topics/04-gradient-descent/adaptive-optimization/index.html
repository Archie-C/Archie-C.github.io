Adaptive optimisation.

Can previous gradients help us adjust the scale of updates for individual parameters?

What this note covers

  • Coordinate-wise scaling and AdaGrad
  • Moving averages in RMSProp and Adam
  • Bias correction and the complete Adam update
  • Comparing optimiser behaviour and practical trade-offs

Why adapt the update?

In the previous note, different curvatures imposed conflicting requirements on a single learning rate. Ordinary gradient descent uses $\mathbf w_{t+1}=\mathbf w_t-\eta\nabla E(\mathbf w_t)$. Gradient components can have different magnitudes, but every component is multiplied by the same $\eta$.

Consider the exact quadratic

$$E(w_1,w_2)=\tfrac12(100w_1^2+w_2^2),\qquad \nabla E=\begin{pmatrix}100w_1\\w_2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf H=\begin{pmatrix}100&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

The steep direction has curvature 100 and the shallow direction curvature 1. As in the previous note, convergence from arbitrary starting points requires $0<\eta<2/100$. Yet in the shallow direction the error is multiplied by $1-\eta$ each step: even a rate close to the upper bound leaves more than 98% of that error after one step. A rate small enough for the steep direction can therefore make slow progress along the valley.

What if each coordinate had its own scale? We could write $w_{i,t+1}=w_{i,t}-\eta_{i,t}\partial E/\partial w_i$. Adaptive methods use previous gradients to adjust such scales during training. They generally do not calculate the Hessian or discover an optimal rate: gradient statistics provide practical heuristics. Scaling coordinates separately is especially natural for an axis-aligned bowl; when curvature directions are rotated, it does not capture all interactions between parameters.

AdaGrad

Suppose one parameter repeatedly receives large gradients, while another receives small or infrequent gradients. AdaGrad records the squared gradients separately for each parameter, reducing the scale of subsequent updates where substantial gradients have accumulated. For a mini-batch $\mathcal B_t$, define the gradient at the previous parameters as $\mathbf g_t=\nabla E_{\mathcal B_t}(\mathbf w_{t-1})$. Starting with $\mathbf r_0=\mathbf0$, AdaGrad computes

$$\mathbf r_t=\mathbf r_{t-1}+\mathbf g_t\odot\mathbf g_t,\qquad \boxed{\mathbf w_t=\mathbf w_{t-1}-\eta\frac{\mathbf g_t}{\sqrt{\mathbf r_t}+\epsilon}.}$$

Multiplication, square roots and division are element-wise. Squaring removes the sign, so positive and negative gradients both add to the history instead of cancelling. The square root brings the accumulated quantity back to gradient units: without it, scaling all gradients would affect the update differently. A small positive $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero and stabilises very small denominators. Here it is added outside the square root; other formulations place it inside, which gives a different numerical rule, particularly for small estimates. Each coordinate has a separate history because different parameters can experience different gradient magnitudes and frequencies.

For coordinate $i$, the rule and its effective learning rate are

$$w_{i,t}=w_{i,t-1}-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{r_{i,t}}+\epsilon}g_{i,t},\qquad \eta_{i,t}=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{r_{i,t}}+\epsilon}.$$

A larger accumulated squared gradient gives a smaller multiplier for the current gradient. The multiplier is not the update itself: the current gradient still determines its sign and contributes to its magnitude.

Worked example

Let $g_1=2$, $g_2=4$, $g_3=2$, with $r_0=0$ and $\eta=0.1$. Take $\epsilon=0$ only for this example, since all denominators are positive; an implementation still needs a positive value.

$$\begin{aligned}r_1&=2^2=4,&\Delta w_1&=-0.1(2/\sqrt4)=-0.1,\\r_2&=4+4^2=20,&\Delta w_2&=-0.1(4/\sqrt{20})\approx-0.08944,\\r_3&=20+2^2=24,&\Delta w_3&=-0.1(2/\sqrt{24})\approx-0.04082.\end{aligned}$$

The third gradient equals the first, but its update is smaller because the accumulated history is larger. In fact $r_{i,t}=\sum_{k=1}^t g_{i,k}^2$ never decreases, so the effective rate cannot increase when $\eta$ is fixed. If $|g_{i,k}|\approx c>0$ throughout, then $r_{i,t}\approx tc^2$ and, once $\epsilon$ is negligible relative to $c\sqrt t$, $\eta_{i,t}\approx\eta/(c\sqrt t)$. This is an approximation under those assumptions, not a universal rate for arbitrary gradients.

During long training the accumulating denominator can make updates very small. A decreasing scale is not always undesirable: for sparse features, coordinates that have rarely received gradients accumulate less history and can retain relatively larger effective rates when an informative gradient arrives.

RMSProp

AdaGrad never forgets a squared gradient. RMSProp replaces the sum with an exponentially weighted moving average, so that the scale can adjust when gradient magnitudes change:

$$\boxed{\mathbf r_t=\beta\mathbf r_{t-1}+(1-\beta)\mathbf g_t^2},\qquad \boxed{\mathbf w_t=\mathbf w_{t-1}-\eta\frac{\mathbf g_t}{\sqrt{\mathbf r_t}+\epsilon}},\quad 0<\beta<1.$$

With $\mathbf r_0=\mathbf0$, repeated substitution gives $\mathbf r_t=(1-\beta)\sum_{k=1}^t\beta^{t-k}\mathbf g_k^2$. The newest squared gradient has weight $1-\beta$; each step into the past multiplies the weight by another $\beta$. Old information gradually fades. A value near zero follows the latest squared gradient closely; a moderately large value averages over more steps; a value very close to one is smoother but responds slowly to changes. A common example is $\beta=0.9$.

For the same $2,4,2$ sequence, with $\beta=0.5$ and $r_0=0$,

$$r_1=0.5(0)+0.5(4)=2,\quad r_2=0.5(2)+0.5(16)=9,\quad r_3=0.5(9)+0.5(4)=6.5.$$

The estimate can fall as well as rise: the last squared gradient is below the preceding estimate. With $\eta=0.1$ and $\epsilon=0$ for this calculation, the updates are approximately $-0.14142$, $-0.13333$ and $-0.07845$. The effective rates are approximately $0.07071$, $0.03333$ and $0.03922$, compared with AdaGrad's $0.05$, $0.02236$ and $0.02041$. Equal numerical hyperparameters do not make this a performance comparison: one statistic is a sum and the other a normalised moving average. RMSProp avoids an indefinitely growing sum, but does not guarantee that every finite or stochastic step reduces the loss.

Adam: adaptive moment estimation

RMSProp scales the current gradient using recent squared gradients. Momentum, by contrast, retains signed gradient contributions to influence the direction of an update. Adam combines related ideas, but is not simply classical momentum followed by RMSProp. It maintains its own two moving averages, initially zero:

$$\mathbf m_t=\beta_1\mathbf m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf g_t,\qquad \mathbf v_t=\beta_2\mathbf v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)\mathbf g_t^2.$$

The first moment $\mathbf m_t$ averages signed gradients: consistent directions reinforce one another, while alternating signs can cancel. The second raw moment $\mathbf v_t$ averages squared gradients; it is not the variance, which also subtracts the square of the mean. The parameters $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ control the memory of these different statistics. Larger values give smoother, slower-changing estimates. Common starting values are $\beta_1=0.9$ and $\beta_2=0.999$; Bishop also discusses $\beta_2=0.99$. None is universally optimal.

After correcting their initialisation bias, Adam uses

$$\boxed{\mathbf w_t=\mathbf w_{t-1}-\eta\frac{\hat{\mathbf m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf v}_t}+\epsilon}.}$$

The numerator supplies a smoothed direction, and the denominator scales each coordinate according to its recent squared-gradient magnitude. Because the numerator includes history, it need not have the sign of the current gradient. The two states serve different purposes and require separate values per parameter.

Why correct the bias?

Both averages start at zero. Early on, their weights on observed gradients have not yet accumulated to one. Expanding the first recurrence gives

$$\mathbf m_t=(1-\beta_1)\sum_{k=1}^t\beta_1^{t-k}\mathbf g_k,\qquad (1-\beta_1)\sum_{k=1}^t\beta_1^{t-k}=1-\beta_1^t.$$

If the gradient process has a constant expectation $\boldsymbol\mu_g$, linearity of expectation gives $\mathbb E[\mathbf m_t]=(1-\beta_1^t)\boldsymbol\mu_g$. Thus, relative to that stationary mean, zero initialisation pulls the estimate towards zero. Dividing by the weight sum removes this initialisation bias. The same argument for $\mathbf v_t$ assumes a constant expected squared gradient:

$$\boxed{\hat{\mathbf m}_t=\frac{\mathbf m_t}{1-\beta_1^t}},\qquad \boxed{\hat{\mathbf v}_t=\frac{\mathbf v_t}{1-\beta_2^t}}.$$

These corrections do not make the averages unbiased for every changing gradient process. When a decay parameter is close to one, its early weight sum can be particularly small; as $t$ grows, both powers approach zero and both divisors approach one. The correction therefore has its greatest effect early in training.

Worked example

Use prescribed gradients $g_1=2$, $g_2=4$, $\beta_1=\beta_2=0.5$, $\eta=0.1$ and $m_0=v_0=0$. These gradients illustrate the internal calculations; they are not successive evaluations of a specified loss. At the first step:

$$\begin{aligned}m_1&=0.5(0)+0.5(2)=1,&v_1&=0.5(0)+0.5(2^2)=2,\\\hat m_1&=1/(1-0.5)=2,&\hat v_1&=2/(1-0.5)=4.\end{aligned}$$

Ignoring $\epsilon$ for these positive denominators, $\Delta w_1=-0.1(2/\sqrt4)=-0.1$. At the second step the new gradient contributes to both histories:

$$\begin{aligned}m_2&=0.5(1)+0.5(4)=2.5,&v_2&=0.5(2)+0.5(16)=9,\\\hat m_2&=2.5/(1-0.5^2)=10/3,&\hat v_2&=9/(1-0.5^2)=12,\\\Delta w_2&=-0.1\frac{10/3}{\sqrt{12}}\approx-0.096225.\end{aligned}$$

The first average retains the signed earlier gradient, whereas the second retains its square. Both corrected values enter the final update; changing either decay parameter changes how much history each retains.

The Adam algorithm

At each iteration, calculate the mini-batch gradient at the current parameters, update both moment estimates, correct their initialisation bias using the current iteration number, then update the parameters. Starting with $t=0$ and $\mathbf m_0=\mathbf v_0=\mathbf0$:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf g_t&=\nabla E_{\mathcal B_t}(\mathbf w_{t-1}),\\\mathbf m_t&=\beta_1\mathbf m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf g_t,&\mathbf v_t&=\beta_2\mathbf v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)\mathbf g_t^2,\\\hat{\mathbf m}_t&=\frac{\mathbf m_t}{1-\beta_1^t},&\hat{\mathbf v}_t&=\frac{\mathbf v_t}{1-\beta_2^t},\\\mathbf w_t&=\mathbf w_{t-1}-\eta\frac{\hat{\mathbf m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf v}_t}+\epsilon}.\end{aligned}$$

All squares, square roots and divisions are element-wise. A compact description of the same order is:

initialise w, m = 0, v = 0, t = 0
repeat:
    t = t + 1
    g = gradient of mini-batch loss at w
    m = β₁ m + (1 − β₁) g
    v = β₂ v + (1 − β₂) g²
    m_hat = m / (1 − β₁ᵗ)
    v_hat = v / (1 − β₂ᵗ)
    w = w − η m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + ε)

Adam stores two additional arrays with the same shape as the parameters and performs element-wise arithmetic on them each step. This increases memory and computation compared with ordinary gradient descent, though it does not require storing a full Hessian.

Comparing optimisers

All five methods can use the same current gradient, but differ in what they retain. In classical momentum the retained velocity combines previous updates; in Adam the first moment is a normalised moving average of gradients. The table describes the forms used in the figure.

Each method has a learning rate; momentum adds $\mu$, RMSProp adds $\beta$, and Adam adds $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$. The same numerical rate does not produce equivalent updates. A meaningful performance comparison can require tuning each method separately and considering computation as well as loss, rather than counting a few steps at one shared rate.

Practical considerations

Adaptive rates do not remove the need to choose $\eta$: it sets the overall scale and can still cause slow progress or unstable steps. The decay parameters govern a trade-off between smooth estimates and responsiveness. $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero and limits amplification when a squared-gradient estimate is very small. Sparse gradients can make per-coordinate histories useful when parameters receive informative updates at different frequencies.

Neural network losses are generally non-convex. None of these algorithms guarantees a global minimum in that setting, and a lower training loss or faster initial progress does not by itself imply better performance on unseen data. Generalisation depends on the model, data, hyperparameters and training procedure. SGD with momentum and Adam are both widely used: they differ in state, sensitivity and behaviour, so a suitable choice depends on the problem and empirical validation.

Coordinate-wise adaptation does not model all cross-parameter interactions in the Hessian, especially when its principal directions do not align with the axes. AdamW is a commonly used Adam variant that decouples weight decay from the adaptive gradient update; regularisation will be considered later.

Summary

AdaGrad divides each gradient component by the root of its accumulated squared history. RMSProp replaces the ever-growing sum with a moving average. Adam combines a signed moving average with a squared-gradient average and corrects their zero-initialisation bias. These mechanisms change how updates are constructed, but their effectiveness depends on the objective and hyperparameters.
