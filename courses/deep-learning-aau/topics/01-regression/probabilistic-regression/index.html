Regression as Probabilistic Modelling.

Squared error can be understood as a likelihood objective: it follows from a model that treats each target as a noisy observation around the regression prediction.

From a prediction to a distribution

A regression function gives a point prediction for an input. For an input vector $\mathbf{x}$ and parameters $\mathbf{w}$, write this prediction as $y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})$. An observed target $t$ will rarely equal it exactly, so model the difference as observation noise:

$$ t = y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}) + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2). $$

Adding Gaussian noise to a fixed prediction means that the target has a Gaussian conditional distribution:

$$ p(t\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(t\mid y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}),\sigma^2\right). $$

Here $y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})$ is the conditional mean: the average target predicted for inputs like $\mathbf{x}$ under this model. The variance $\sigma^2$ describes the assumed spread of observations around that mean. The model therefore predicts a distribution of possible target values, even when we report only its mean.

The likelihood of a dataset

Now consider $N$ observed input-target pairs, $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_n,t_n)\}_{n=1}^{N}$. Assume targets are conditionally independent given their inputs and the model parameters. The joint density of the observed target vector $\mathbf{t}$ is then the product of the individual Gaussian densities:

$$ p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal{N}\!\left(t_n\mid y(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{w}),\sigma^2\right). $$

As a probability density, this expression describes how density is distributed over possible datasets when $\mathbf{X}$, $\mathbf{w}$, and $\sigma^2$ are fixed. Once the dataset has been observed, we can instead regard the same expression as a likelihood function: the data are held fixed, and we ask which values of $\mathbf{w}$ make those observations most plausible under the model. We may also vary $\sigma^2$, though for now we keep it fixed.

From likelihood to squared error

Write $\hat{t}_n = y(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{w})$ for the prediction of the $n$th target. We now take the logarithm of the dataset density. The log of a product is the sum of the logs:

$$ \begin{gathered} \log p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) \\ = \log \prod_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal{N}\!\left(t_n\mid\hat{t}_n,\sigma^2\right) \\ = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log \mathcal{N}\!\left(t_n\mid\hat{t}_n,\sigma^2\right) \end{gathered} $$

For one observation, the Gaussian density is a normalising factor multiplied by an exponential that penalises its squared residual:

$$ \begin{gathered} \mathcal{N}\!\left(t_n\mid\hat{t}_n,\sigma^2\right) \\ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \\ \qquad\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{(t_n-\hat{t}_n)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right). \end{gathered} $$

Taking its logarithm turns the multiplication into addition. The logarithm of the exponential returns its exponent, and the logarithm of the normalising factor becomes $-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$:

$$ \begin{gathered} \log \mathcal{N}\!\left(t_n\mid\hat{t}_n,\sigma^2\right) \\ = \log\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\right) \\ \quad + \log\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\frac{(t_n-\hat{t}_n)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\right] \\ = -\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \\ -\frac{(t_n-\hat{t}_n)^2}{2\sigma^2}. \end{gathered} $$

Substitute the single-observation log density into the sum over all $N$ observations. Separating the normalising term from the residual term gives:

$$ \begin{gathered} \log p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) \\ = -\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \\ -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(t_n-\hat{t}_n)^2 \end{gathered} $$

The normalising term is identical for every observation, so its sum is $-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$. The residual terms share the factor $1/(2\sigma^2)$, which is why it sits outside their sum:

$$ \begin{gathered} \log p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) \\ = -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \\ -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(t_n-\hat{t}_n)^2 \end{gathered} $$

Finally, split $\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$ using $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$:

$$ \begin{gathered} \log p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) \\ = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(t_n-\hat{t}_n)^2 \\ -\frac{N}{2}\log\sigma^2-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi). \end{gathered} $$

To find the maximum-likelihood parameters $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ML}}$, maximize this log likelihood. With $\sigma^2$ fixed, the last two terms are constants with respect to $\mathbf{w}$, so they cannot change which weights give the maximum. The remaining term is the negative of the sum of squared residuals multiplied by the positive constant $1/(2\sigma^2)$. Multiplying an objective by a positive constant does not change its minimizer, and maximizing log likelihood is equivalent to minimizing negative log likelihood. Therefore:

$$ \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ML}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(y(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{w})-t_n\right)^2. $$

The factor $1/2$ is also a positive constant, often included because it simplifies derivatives. Using the mean squared error instead of the sum only divides by the fixed number of observations $N$, so it has the same optimum.

What assumptions did we make?

This result follows from a specific observation model. It assumes:

  1. The target is continuous, so a Gaussian density is a suitable model for its values.
  2. Errors are Gaussian around the model prediction.
  3. Errors have mean zero, so the prediction is the conditional mean.
  4. The variance $\sigma^2$ is the same for every input, an assumption called homoscedastic noise.
  5. Observations are conditionally independent given their inputs and the model.

Change the assumed noise distribution and the likelihood, and therefore the loss, changes too. For example, Laplace-distributed errors lead to an absolute-error objective. The squared-error result is not universal: it is the consequence of the Gaussian, constant-variance assumptions above.

What does the model predict?

The value $y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})$ is the predicted conditional mean. The distribution $p(t\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2)$ describes possible target values around that mean under the assumed noise model. Ordinary least-squares regression often reports only the mean prediction, while its usual squared-error objective can be understood as fitting the mean by maximum likelihood under Gaussian observation noise.

key takeaway

Squared error is the negative log-likelihood, up to constants and a positive scale, for independent Gaussian observation noise with fixed variance. A different noise model can imply a different loss.
