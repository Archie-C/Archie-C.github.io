Jacobians, Hessians, and higher-order derivatives.

Extend gradient calculations to sensitivity and curvature.

What this note covers

  • The Jacobian
  • The Hessian
  • Hessian–vector products
  • A curvature approximation

The Jacobian

For $\hat{\mathbf{y}}=f(\mathbf x)$ with $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^d$ and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb R^q$, the Jacobian collects the sensitivities of every output to every input:

$$\mathbf{J}_{ki}=\frac{\partial \hat{y}_k}{\partial x_i},\qquad \mathbf{J}\in\mathbb R^{q\times d}.$$

For a differentiable function and a small input change, $\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}\approx \mathbf{J}\Delta\mathbf x$. The $i$th column describes the first-order effect of changing input $i$ alone. For example, let $f(x_1,x_2)=(x_1^2+x_2,\ x_1x_2)^\top$. Then

$$\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}2x_1&1\\x_2&x_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{J}(1,2)=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

At this point an input change $(0.01,0)^\top$ gives the first-order prediction $(0.02,0.02)^\top$ for the change in output. A full Jacobian is useful when all local sensitivities are needed, but it can be large. Forward-mode AD calculates $\mathbf{J}\mathbf v$ for an input direction $\mathbf v$; reverse-mode AD calculates $\mathbf u^\top \mathbf{J}$ for output weights $\mathbf u$. Neither product requires the whole matrix to be stored. To construct the full Jacobian, seed forward mode once per input coordinate to obtain its columns, or seed reverse mode once per output coordinate to obtain its rows. The local sensitivities generally depend on the input, so the Jacobian must be evaluated at the point of interest.

The Hessian

For a twice-differentiable scalar objective $L(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, with $P$ parameters collected in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, the Hessian is the Jacobian of its gradient:

$$\mathbf{H}_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \theta_i\partial \theta_j},\qquad \mathbf{H}\in\mathbb R^{P\times P}.$$

Its quadratic form describes local curvature. If $L$ is sufficiently smooth,

$$L(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx L(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta})^\top\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}+\frac12\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \mathbf{H}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}.$$

For example, $L(\theta_1,\theta_2)=\theta_1^2+2\theta_1\theta_2+3\theta_2^2$ has gradient $(2\theta_1+2\theta_2,\,2\theta_1+6\theta_2)^\top$ and Hessian

$$\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&6\end{pmatrix}.$$

The off-diagonal entries capture how the gradient in one coordinate changes with the other. Curvature informs second-order optimisation and the discussion of convergence. A full Hessian has $P^2$ entries and requires $O(P^2)$ storage. Under the usual linear-work assumptions for a standard network, obtaining all its entries by differentiating the gradient also takes $O(P^2)$ work. Both costs are generally prohibitive for a large network.

When the mixed second derivatives are continuous, their order can be exchanged and the Hessian is symmetric. Along a unit direction $\mathbf v$, the scalar $\mathbf v^\top\mathbf H\mathbf v$ measures the second-order change in that direction. Positive curvature bends the loss upwards along that direction; negative curvature bends it downwards. At a stationary point, a positive definite Hessian is sufficient for a strict local minimum, while an indefinite Hessian indicates a saddle point. A zero eigenvalue requires further analysis.

The example Hessian is positive definite: its leading principal minors are $2$ and $8$. Its curvature is nevertheless different in different directions. This helps explain why a single learning rate can make progress at different speeds along different directions, as seen in the earlier gradient-descent notes.

Hessian–vector products

Often we need the action of the Hessian on a direction $\mathbf v$, not its entries. Since $\nabla L$ is a vector function, $\mathbf{H}\mathbf v$ is its directional derivative. AD can differentiate the gradient computation in direction $\mathbf v$, without forming $\mathbf{H}$. For a standard smooth network with $O(P)$ operations, an appropriate forward-over-reverse computation has $O(P)$ work up to constant factors, with memory costs depending on implementation. Products are useful for probing curvature or iterative second-order methods.

A curvature approximation

As an extension, consider one example with squared-error loss $L=\frac12\sum_k r_k(\boldsymbol{\theta})^2$, where $k$ indexes output components, with residuals $r_k=\hat{y}_k-y_k$ and residual Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_r$, differentiating twice gives

$$\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{J}_r^\top \mathbf{J}_r+\sum_k r_k\nabla^2 r_k.$$

The Gauss–Newton approximation retains $\mathbf{J}_r^\top \mathbf{J}_r$, a sum of outer products of residual gradients, and drops the second term. It is positive semidefinite and can be useful when residuals are small or the omitted curvature is otherwise negligible. It need not equal the true Hessian away from those conditions and cannot represent negative curvature. Generalised Gauss–Newton constructions extend the idea to other suitable losses.
