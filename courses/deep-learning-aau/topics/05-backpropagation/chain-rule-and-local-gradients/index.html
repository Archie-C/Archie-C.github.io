The chain rule and local gradients.

Combine local derivatives to calculate the effect of a parameter on the loss.

What this note covers

  • The chain rule
  • Computational graphs and branching
  • A single-layer network

The chain rule

Suppose $u=f(x)$ and $v=g(u)$. A small change in $x$ changes $u$ by approximately $f'(x)\Delta x$, and that change in $u$ changes $v$ by approximately $g'(u)\Delta u$. Combining the two local effects gives $\Delta v\approx g'(u)f'(x)\Delta x$. Taking the limit for differentiable functions gives

$$\frac{dv}{dx}=\frac{dv}{du}\frac{du}{dx}=g'(f(x))f'(x).$$

For example, if $u=x^2$ and $v=\sin u$, then $dv/dx=\cos(x^2)2x$. At $x=1$, the result is $2\cos 1\approx1.0806$. For a longer sequence, multiply the local derivatives along the sequence.

For three transformations, $u=f(x)$, $v=g(u)$ and $q=h(v)$, the same argument applies twice:

$$\frac{dq}{dx}=\frac{dq}{dv}\frac{dv}{du}\frac{du}{dx}.$$

Each factor is evaluated at the value reached during the forward calculation. For example, if $u=2x$, $v=u+1$ and $q=v^2$, the local derivatives are $2$, $1$ and $2v$. At $x=1$, the intermediate values are $u=2$ and $v=3$, so $dq/dx=6\cdot1\cdot2=12$. Recording intermediate values lets us evaluate the local derivatives without rebuilding the whole expression.

Computational graphs and branching

A computational graph records each intermediate calculation as a node. Its directed edges show which values are needed to calculate another value. Local derivatives describe how each operation responds to its inputs.

Let $u=x^2$, $v=3x$ and $q=u+v$. Changing $x$ changes both inputs of the addition, so the multivariable chain rule adds their effects:

$$\frac{dq}{dx}=\frac{\partial q}{\partial u}\frac{du}{dx}+\frac{\partial q}{\partial v}\frac{dv}{dx}=1(2x)+1(3)=2x+3.$$

At $x=2$ the two paths contribute $4$ and $3$, giving $7$. This agrees with differentiating $q=x^2+3x$ directly. The sum is essential: ignoring either path would miss part of the effect of $x$.

A single-layer network

Consider a linear output $\hat{y}_j=\sum_i w_{ji}x_i$ and the loss $L=\frac12\sum_j(\hat{y}_j-y_j)^2$, where $y_j$ is the target for output $j$ and $\hat{y}_j$ is its prediction. The loss $L$ is for this one example (equivalently the empirical risk of a one-example dataset). The weight $w_{ji}$ affects $L$ through output $\hat{y}_j$. Holding the input and other weights fixed,

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ji}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}_j}\frac{\partial \hat{y}_j}{\partial w_{ji}}=(\hat{y}_j-y_j)x_i.$$

To see the two local derivatives separately, only the $j$th term in the loss depends on $\hat{y}_j$, and only the $i$th term in the weighted sum depends on $w_{ji}$. Thus $\partial L/\partial \hat{y}_j=\frac12\cdot2(\hat{y}_j-y_j)=\hat{y}_j-y_j$ and $\partial \hat{y}_j/\partial w_{ji}=x_i$. The other output terms and other weights are held fixed in these partial derivatives.

The first factor is the output error signal; the second is the activation entering the connection. Their product tells us how sensitive the loss is to that weight. If $x_i=2$, $\hat{y}_j=3$ and $y_j=1$, then the gradient is $(3-1)2=4$. A small increase in the weight increases the loss locally by about four times that increase.

For a hidden unit there is no target for its activation. Its error signal must instead be obtained from its effects on downstream units. The next note derives that calculation.
