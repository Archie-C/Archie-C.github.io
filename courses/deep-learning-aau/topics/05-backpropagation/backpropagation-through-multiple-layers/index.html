Backpropagation through multiple layers.

Derive the error signals and gradients for a feed-forward network.

What this note covers

A feed-forward network

Following the notation reference, $z_j$ is the weighted input, or pre-activation, and $a_j$ is the activation. We first consider one training example, so its loss is $L=\ell(\hat{y},y)$; this is also the empirical risk for a dataset of one example. For unit $j$,

$$z_j=\sum_i w_{ji}a_i+b_j,\qquad a_j=\sigma(z_j).$$

Here $w_{ji}$ connects unit $i$ to unit $j$, $b_j$ is a bias, and $\sigma$ is the activation function. An input unit supplies $a_i=x_i$. The formulas also describe a feed-forward graph with skip connections: the sum includes all direct predecessors. We assume differentiable operations at the values considered; at a ReLU kink a framework chooses a convention for the derivative.

Error signals and parameter gradients

Define the error signal at a unit as $\delta_j\equiv\partial L/\partial z_j$. For a weight entering that unit, $\partial z_j/\partial w_{ji}=a_i$. The chain rule therefore gives

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ji}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial w_{ji}}=\delta_j a_i},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_j}=\delta_j.$$

The bias derivative follows because $\partial z_j/\partial b_j=1$. This extends the single-layer result: the incoming activation is multiplied by the error signal at the receiving unit.

Hidden-layer recursion

A hidden activation $a_j$ can affect each downstream unit $k$ connected to it. Since $\partial z_k/\partial a_j=w_{kj}$, the contributions of all these paths must be added. As $\partial a_j/\partial z_j=\sigma'(z_j)$,

$$\begin{aligned}\delta_j=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}&=\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial z_j}\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j}\\&=\sigma'(z_j)\sum_k\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k}\frac{\partial z_k}{\partial a_j}\\&=\boxed{\sigma'(z_j)\sum_k w_{kj}\delta_k}.\end{aligned}$$

The weights describe how strongly each downstream pre-activation depends on $a_j$. Their weighted error signals add because the paths all affect the loss. The activation derivative then converts the derivative with respect to $a_j$ into one with respect to $z_j$. We need the downstream signals first, so the calculation proceeds backwards. In a general directed acyclic graph, the sum covers all direct successors, and operations with several inputs use their corresponding local derivatives.

For a hidden unit with two successors, the sum makes the accumulation explicit: $\partial L/\partial a_j=w_{1j}\delta_1+w_{2j}\delta_2$. Each term answers a different question: how does changing $a_j$ affect the loss through that successor? A negative weight can reverse a contribution, and the two contributions can reinforce or cancel. Only after adding them do we multiply by $\sigma'(z_j)$ to obtain the signal at the pre-activation.

The recursion assumes each activation is an elementwise function of its own pre-activation. If an operation couples coordinates, such as softmax or normalisation, its local derivative is a matrix and contributions from all coupled coordinates must be included. Reverse-mode differentiation still applies, but the simple elementwise formula is not sufficient for that operation.

Starting at the output

At the output we differentiate the chosen loss and output activation. For independent output activations, $\delta_k=(\partial L/\partial \hat{y}_k)\sigma'_\mathrm{out}(z_k)$. With a linear output and $L=\frac12\sum_k(\hat{y}_k-y_k)^2$, this gives $\delta_k=\hat{y}_k-y_k$. The same simplification holds for a sigmoid with binary cross-entropy, and for softmax with categorical cross-entropy when the target components sum to one. For softmax the outputs are coupled, so the full multivariable derivative is required before simplifying. Other choices of loss, activation or reduction can give a different signal.

For example, take one sigmoid output $\hat{p}=\sigma(z)$ and binary cross-entropy $L=-y\log \hat{p}-(1-y)\log(1-\hat{p})$, with $y\in\{0,1\}$. The local derivatives are

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{p}}=-\frac{y}{\hat{p}}+\frac{1-y}{1-\hat{p}},\qquad \frac{\partial \hat{p}}{\partial z}=\hat{p}(1-\hat{p}).$$

Multiplying them gives $\delta=\partial L/\partial z=\hat{p}-y$. This cancellation depends on the particular pair of functions. For categorical cross-entropy $L=-\sum_i y_i\log \hat{p}_i$ and softmax, the outputs are coupled: $\partial \hat{p}_i/\partial z_k=\hat{p}_i(\mathbb{1}_{i=k}-\hat{p}_k)$. Consequently,

$$\delta_k=\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{p}_i}\frac{\partial \hat{p}_i}{\partial z_k}= -\sum_i y_i(\mathbb{1}_{i=k}-\hat{p}_k)=\hat{p}_k\sum_i y_i-y_k.$$

When $\sum_i y_i=1$, this is $\hat{p}_k-y_k$. The same derivation also works for probability-valued targets with unit sum. A mean over outputs or examples introduces its corresponding scale factor.

Matrix form

For a layered network with widths $d_0,\ldots,d_L$, take $\mathbf{a}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x}$ and

$$\mathbf{z}^{(l)}=\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)},\qquad \mathbf{a}^{(l)}=\sigma^{(l)}(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}).$$

$\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$ has shape $d_l\times d_{l-1}$, and $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$, $\mathbf{a}^{(l)}$, $\mathbf{b}^{(l)}$ and $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ are column vectors of length $d_l$. For elementwise hidden activations, the scalar recursion becomes

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}=\big((\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)})^\top\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}\big)\odot (\sigma^{(l)})'(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}),$$

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)})^\top,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}.$$

The transpose maps the $d_{l+1}$ downstream signals back to $d_l$ units. The elementwise product $\odot$ applies each local activation slope. The outer product has shape $d_l\times d_{l-1}$, with entry $\delta_j^{(l)}a_i^{(l-1)}$, exactly matching its weight matrix. Repeat the recursion for every hidden layer.

Putting the steps together

  1. Evaluate the network forwards and retain the required intermediate values.
  2. Calculate the loss from predictions and targets.
  3. Differentiate the loss and output activation to initialise the output signals.
  4. Propagate those signals backwards through each hidden layer.
  5. Calculate each weight and bias gradient from its signal and input.
  6. Give the gradients to the optimiser, which can then update the parameters.

When each prediction depends only on its own example, differentiate each example's loss and add the resulting gradients. If the mini-batch objective is the mean of $B$ example losses, divide this sum by $B$; if it is the sum, do not divide. Any averaging over output dimensions also changes the scale. Regularisation terms contribute their own derivatives. With training-time batch normalisation, examples also influence one another through the batch statistics; differentiate the complete batch computation rather than running isolated examples. The mini-batch note discusses how this objective relates to an update.
