    Question 1

    View question 1 ↗

    A binary classifier predicts class A when $z(\mathbf{x}) = b + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 > 0$, with $b=-1$, $w_1=2$, $w_2=-3$.

    1. Predict the class for $\mathbf{x}=(1,1)$ and $\mathbf{x}=(0,1)$.

      Substituting the weights gives:

      $$ z(\mathbf{x}) = -1 + 2x_1 - 3x_2. $$

      $$ \begin{aligned} z(1,1) &= -1 + 2 - 3 = -2 < 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{not A}, \\ z(0,1) &= -1 + 0 - 3 = -4 < 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{not A}. \end{aligned} $$

      Answering "B" is fine, but the question never names the other class, so "not A" is more precise.

    2. Derive the decision boundary.

      The boundary is where the score is exactly zero:

      $$ -1 + 2x_1 - 3x_2 = 0. $$

      That is the boundary, but it is easier to interpret once rearranged for either $x_1$ or $x_2$:

      $$ x_2 = \frac{2x_1 - 1}{3}, \qquad x_1 = \frac{3x_2 + 1}{2}. $$

      So the boundary is a straight line with gradient $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and $x_2$-intercept $-\tfrac{1}{3}$.

    3. Explain geometrically what $b$ and $\mathbf{w}$ control.

      The vector $\mathbf{w}$ sets the orientation of the boundary: it is perpendicular to the boundary and points towards the region classified as A. For a fixed $\mathbf{w}$, the bias $b$ sets where the boundary sits in space.

      This is the familiar hyperplane equation $\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n} = k$, with $\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{w}$ and $k=-b$. The perpendicular distance from the origin to the boundary is $|b|/\|\mathbf{w}\|$.

    4. What happens if only $b$ is increased?

      The boundary moves parallel to itself: its orientation, set by $\mathbf{w}$, is unchanged. Changing $b$ changes the perpendicular distance $|b|/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ from the origin to the boundary.

      Here, increasing $b$ shifts the line in the direction of $-\mathbf{w}$, so more of the input space is classified as A.

    Question 2

    View question 2 ↗

    A defect detector is tested on 1000 components: 20 are defective, 12 are detected, and 30 non-defective components are wrongly flagged.

    1. Construct a confusion matrix.

      Treat "defective" as the positive class. There are 20 defects and 12 are detected, so $TP=12$ and $FN=20-12=8$. 30 good components are flagged, so $FP=30$, which leaves $TN=1000-12-8-30=950$.

      As in the notes, actual classes are in the rows and predicted classes in the columns:

      Actual \ PredictedDefectiveNon-defective
      DefectiveTP = 12FN = 8
      Non-defectiveFP = 30TN = 950

      Some sources swap the axes. That is fine as long as the axes are labelled.

    2. Calculate accuracy, precision and recall.

      $$ \begin{aligned} \text{Accuracy} &= \frac{TP+TN}{N} = \frac{12+950}{1000} = 96.2\%, \\ \text{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FP} = \frac{12}{42} \approx 28.6\%, \\ \text{Recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = \frac{12}{20} = 60\%. \end{aligned} $$

    3. Accuracy of always predicting "non-defective".

      It correctly classifies all 980 non-defective components and misses all 20 defects:

      $$ \text{Accuracy} = \frac{980}{1000} = 98\%. $$

    4. Why is accuracy a poor metric here?

      A classifier that never detects a single defect scores 98%, which is higher than the real model's 96.2%. The large class imbalance inflates accuracy: almost every observation is non-defective, so predicting the majority class is rewarded even though the classifier is useless for the actual task.

    5. Which metric to prioritise if missed defects are dangerous?

      Letting a defect through is a false negative. To catch every defect, we want to maximise TPs and minimise FNs, so recall is the most informative metric.

      There is a drawback. A classifier that predicts "non-defective" for everything gets 98% accuracy, and one that flags everything as defective gets 100% recall. A better metric here is the F1 score, which balances precision and recall:

      $$ F_1 = \frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}} = \frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN} = \frac{24}{62} \approx 0.39. $$

    Question 3

    Logistic regression trained by maximum likelihood on a perfectly linearly separable binary training set.

    1. What happens to the predicted probabilities when $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ are multiplied by $c>1$?

      The output before the sigmoid is:

      $$ z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b. $$

      Scaling both $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ by $c$ just scales the score:

      $$ (c\mathbf{w})^\top\mathbf{x} + cb = c\,z. $$

      The sign of $z$ does not change, so the decision boundary $z=0$ and every predicted class stay the same. The predicted probability of class 1 is the sigmoid of the score:

      $$ P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(cz) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-cz}}. $$

      If $z>0$, increasing $c$ shrinks $e^{-cz}$ and pushes the probability towards 1. If $z<0$, the opposite happens and it is pushed towards 0. Because the data are separable, every observation has the correct sign, so every prediction becomes more confident and more correct.

    2. What happens to the cross-entropy loss as $c\to\infty$?

      As $c\to\infty$, $p_i\to1$ for every positive observation and $p_i\to0$ for every negative one. The binary cross-entropy is:

      $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big]. $$

      When $y_i=1$, only the first term remains and $\log p_i\to0$. When $y_i=0$, only the second term remains and $\log(1-p_i)\to0$. So, for a perfectly separable training set:

      $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} \to 0. $$

      The loss decreases strictly as $c$ grows but never actually reaches 0.

    3. Why is there no finite maximum-likelihood optimum?

      Maximum likelihood chooses the parameters that maximise the likelihood of the observed labels:

      $$ \text{likelihood} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{\,y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}. $$

      For an observation with $y_i=1$, the second factor has power 0, so its contribution is just $p_i$. For $y_i=0$ it is $1-p_i$. From (a), scaling by $c$ pushes every one of these contributions towards 1, so the product also tends to 1.

      But 1 is only reached in the limit $c\to\infty$. For any finite parameters, scaling them by a larger $c$ gives a strictly higher likelihood (equivalently, a strictly lower cross-entropy). The supremum is never attained, so there is no finite optimum and the weights diverge during training.

    4. Does this mean logistic regression cannot classify all observations correctly?

      No. Not having a finite optimum does not mean the classification is wrong. Any separating $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ already classifies every training observation correctly. The problem is that the optimum is not unique or finite: infinitely many boundaries separate the data, and each one can be made arbitrarily confident.

    5. How does $L_2$ regularisation fix this?

      $L_2$ regularisation adds a penalty on the size of the weights to the loss:

      $$ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2, \qquad \lambda>0. $$

      Now there is a trade-off. Growing the weights still lowers $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$, but the gains shrink towards 0 while the penalty grows without bound. So $\mathcal{L}\to\infty$ as $\|\mathbf{w}\|\to\infty$, and the minimum is reached at finite weights.

    6. Does $L_2$ regularisation necessarily preserve the original decision boundary?

      No. Each weight is penalised independently, so the weights can change relative to each other. That changes the direction of $\mathbf{w}$, and therefore the orientation of the boundary. The boundary's position, $|b|/\|\mathbf{w}\|$, depends on the magnitude of the weights, so it can move too.

      With enough regularisation, the boundary may no longer separate the two classes perfectly.
