Activation functions.

The functions that turn weighted inputs into features, and why their shapes matter.

What this note covers

  • Pre-activations, activations, and their slopes
  • Sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, and leaky ReLU
  • Saturation and zero derivatives
  • Choosing hidden-layer and output-layer activations
  • Comparing the same inputs across activation functions

Why activation functions matter

In the previous note, a neuron took a weighted sum of its inputs, added a bias, and applied an activation function:

$$\phi_j(\mathbf{x};\theta_j)=h(\mathbf{a}_j^\top\mathbf{x}+b_j).$$

Here $\mathbf{a}_j$ contains the neuron's input weights and $b_j$ is its bias. As before, $\mathbf{w}$ contains the separate weights that combine these learned features into a prediction.

The weighted input, including the bias, is $s_j=\mathbf{a}_j^\top\mathbf{x}+b_j$. This is called the pre-activation. The value after applying $h$ is the activation, or neuron output. For example, ReLU turns a pre-activation of $-2$ into an activation of $0$.

Below, $a$ stands for a single pre-activation, such as $s_j$. The function $h(a)$ tells you what the neuron passes to the next layer.

The previous note showed that stacking affine layers, each of the form $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}$, still gives an affine transformation. Nonlinear activation functions allow the network to model nonlinear relationships.

The choice of activation matters. Some functions keep their outputs within a fixed range. Others allow any positive value, or return zero for every negative input. These differences affect both the features a network produces and how it learns.

To compare them, it helps to look at their slopes as well as their outputs. The derivative $h'(a)$ gives the slope at $a$: how much the output changes for a small change in the pre-activation. A slope close to zero means the output barely changes.

The sigmoid activation function

The logistic sigmoid is already familiar from logistic regression:

$$\sigma(a)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}.$$

Sigmoid maps any real input into $(0,1)$. Very negative inputs give outputs close to $0$, and very positive inputs give outputs close to $1$. At zero, the output is halfway between them:

$$\sigma(0)=\frac{1}{1+1}=\frac{1}{2}.$$

This is how sigmoid turned a score into a class probability in logistic regression. It was also widely used in hidden layers, where its output serves as a learned feature.

Its derivative has a useful form:

$$\sigma'(a)=\sigma(a)\bigl(1-\sigma(a)\bigr).$$

At $a=0$, the slope is $\tfrac12(1-\tfrac12)=\tfrac14$, its largest value. As the output approaches $0$ or $1$, the derivative approaches zero and the curve flattens.

This flattening is called saturation. Once a neuron is in this region, changing its pre-activation makes very little difference to its output. That can make learning difficult.

A gradient describes how the loss changes when a model parameter changes. Small activation slopes across several layers can lead to very small gradients in earlier layers. This vanishing gradient problem can slow learning. The details come later; for now, notice the nearly flat ends of the sigmoid curve.

The hyperbolic tangent (tanh)

The hyperbolic tangent, or $\tanh$, has a similar shape to sigmoid, but its outputs can be negative as well as positive:

$$\tanh(a)=\frac{e^a-e^{-a}}{e^a+e^{-a}}.$$

Its output range is $(-1,1)$. Very negative inputs give outputs close to $-1$, and very positive inputs give outputs close to $1$. At zero:

$$\tanh(0)=\frac{1-1}{1+1}=0.$$

Tanh is centred around zero: $\tanh(-a)=-\tanh(a)$. Negative inputs give negative outputs, and positive inputs give positive outputs. You can also express it in terms of sigmoid:

$$\tanh(a)=2\sigma(2a)-1.$$

The derivative is:

$$\frac{d}{da}\tanh(a)=1-\tanh^2(a).$$

At zero, the slope is $1$, compared with $\tfrac14$ for sigmoid. But as the output approaches $-1$ or $1$, its square approaches $1$ and the derivative approaches zero. Tanh also saturates at both ends.

Tanh was often preferred over sigmoid in hidden layers because it produces both negative and positive features. This can make optimisation easier. It still has the same difficulty with saturation.

Rectified linear unit (ReLU)

The rectified linear unit from the previous note uses a much simpler rule:

$$h(a)=\operatorname{ReLU}(a)=\max(0,a).$$

Negative inputs become zero, and positive inputs pass through unchanged. The output at zero is zero. Its range is $[0,\infty)$, with no upper limit. Away from zero, the derivative is:

$$h'(a)=\begin{cases}0 & a<0,\\1 & a>0.\end{cases}$$

At zero, the left-hand slope is $0$ and the right-hand slope is $1$. The derivative is therefore undefined there. Implementations choose a value, usually $0$, for gradient calculations.

ReLU is simple to compute, and its positive side never flattens: the slope stays at $1$. This helps gradients pass through active neurons, even for large positive inputs. Sigmoid and tanh have very small slopes in that region.

The flat negative side causes a different problem. If a neuron has negative pre-activations for every example it sees, its output and activation derivative are always zero. It then receives no gradient through the activation to change its incoming weights or bias. A neuron that stays in this state is called a dead neuron.

Returning zero for one input is normal, as in the previous note's worked example. Other inputs may still give that neuron a positive pre-activation.

Leaky ReLU

Leaky ReLU gives the negative side of ReLU a small slope:

$$h(a)=\begin{cases}a & a\geq0,\\\alpha a & a<0,\end{cases}\qquad 0<\alpha<1.$$

Here $\alpha$ sets the slope for negative inputs. With $\alpha=0.1$, an input of $-2$ produces $-0.2$ instead of zero. Positive inputs pass through unchanged.

The output can now be any real number, so its range is $\mathbb{R}$. Away from zero, its derivative is:

$$h'(a)=\begin{cases}\alpha & a<0,\\1 & a>0.\end{cases}$$

The derivative is now nonzero on the negative side, so a negative pre-activation still allows a gradient through. The derivative is undefined at zero, where the slope changes; implementations choose a value there, as with ReLU.

The slope $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter: a setting you choose before learning. A smaller value makes the negative side flatter. The plots use $\alpha=0.1$ to make this slope easy to see. Some related functions allow the slope to be learned.

Hidden layers and output layers

Hidden layers produce features for the layers that follow them. Their activations make those features nonlinear. At the output layer, the choice depends on what you want to predict.

For regression with unrestricted real-valued targets, an identity output, $h(a)=a$, allows predictions of any size and either sign. It is often called a linear output layer; with a bias, the layer is affine. The hidden layers can still make the overall prediction nonlinear.

For binary classification, sigmoid turns one output score into $p(C_1\mid\mathbf{x})=\sigma(a)$. The other class has probability $1-\sigma(a)$, just as in logistic regression.

For multiclass classification, suppose each example belongs to exactly one of $K$ classes. Let $\mathbf{a}=[a_1,\ldots,a_K]^\top$ contain the output scores, one per class. Softmax turns these scores into class probabilities:

$$\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{a})_k=\frac{e^{a_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{a_j}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.$$

Each exponential is positive. Dividing by their sum makes the outputs add up to one, so output $k$ can represent $p(C_k\mid\mathbf{x})$. Softmax uses all the scores together: changing one score also changes the other probabilities.

Independent sigmoids answer separate yes/no questions, so several labels can be true at once and the probabilities need not add up to one. Softmax is for classes that compete: each example belongs to just one class. This is the distinction from the earlier multiclass classification note.

These choices match the losses from earlier in the course: squared error for regression, binary cross-entropy for binary classification, and multiclass cross-entropy for mutually exclusive classes. Choose the output activation to suit the task; sigmoid and softmax are useful when the prediction should be a probability.

Comparing activation functions

Here are the main differences. For leaky ReLU, $0<\alpha<1$.

Four hidden-layer activation functions
Function Output range Negative / positive inputs Saturation Main tradeoff
Sigmoid $(0,1)$ Below / above $0.5$ Both ends Smooth, bounded outputs; small slopes near the limits.
Tanh $(-1,1)$ Negative / positive Both ends Centred around zero; small slopes near the limits.
ReLU $[0,\infty)$ Zero / unchanged Flat for negative inputs only Slope $1$ for positive inputs; risk of dead neurons.

ReLU is a common starting point for hidden layers: it is simple and does not saturate for positive inputs. The best choice still depends on the architecture and task.

Worked example

Suppose three neurons have pre-activations $-2$, $0$, and $2$. What does each activation function pass to the next layer? For the calculations, use $e^2\approx7.389$ and $e^{-2}\approx0.1353$. For sigmoid:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma(-2)&=\frac{1}{1+e^2}\approx0.119,\\ \sigma(0)&=\frac{1}{2}=0.500,\\ \sigma(2)&=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2}}\approx0.881. \end{aligned}$$

Substituting the same values into tanh gives:

$$\begin{aligned} \tanh(-2)&=\frac{0.1353-7.389}{0.1353+7.389}\approx-0.964,\\ \tanh(0)&=\frac{1-1}{1+1}=0,\\ \tanh(2)&=\frac{7.389-0.1353}{7.389+0.1353}\approx0.964. \end{aligned}$$

ReLU keeps the positive value and replaces the negative value with zero:

$$h(-2)=\max(0,-2)=0,\quad h(0)=0,\quad h(2)=2.$$

The same pre-activations in the three different representations
Pre-activation Sigmoid Tanh ReLU
$-2$$0.119$$-0.964$$0$
$0$$0.500$$0$$0$
$2$$0.881$$0.964$$2$

Sigmoid gives three positive values between $0$ and $1$. Tanh keeps the signs, with magnitudes below $1$. ReLU keeps $2$ but turns $-2$ into zero. The next layer receives different features even though the pre-activations were the same.
